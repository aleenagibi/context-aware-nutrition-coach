## [S1] Daily protein intake for exercising people (ISSN position stand, 2017)
source: Jager et al., J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:20 (2017)
status: checked against the published position statement
For most people who exercise, 1.4 to 2.0 g of protein per kg of body weight per day is enough to build or keep muscle, as long as total energy intake is adequate. During calorie deficits, lean resistance-trained people may need more, around 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg per day, to hold on to lean mass.

## [S8] Protein per meal and timing (ISSN position stand, 2017)
source: Jager et al., J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:20 (2017)
status: checked against the published position statement
A serving of about 0.25 g of high-quality protein per kg body weight, or roughly 20 to 40 g, is suggested to stimulate muscle protein synthesis. Spread servings evenly through the day, about every 3 to 4 hours. Protein before or after resistance training helps; the training effect lasts at least 24 hours but fades with time.

## [S2] The RDA is a minimum, not a training target
source: Dietary reference intakes (RDA 0.8 g/kg/day); ICMR-NIN 2020 gives a similar adult baseline
status: unchecked
The standard RDA of about 0.8 g protein per kg per day is set to prevent deficiency in the general population. It is not designed to optimise muscle gain or recovery, so people who train regularly are usually advised to eat more than this.

## [S3] Where extra protein stops helping muscle gain (Morton meta-analysis, 2018)
source: Morton et al., Br J Sports Med (2018)
status: unchecked
A meta-analysis of resistance-training trials found that protein supplementation modestly improved gains in muscle size and strength. Benefits levelled off at roughly 1.6 g per kg per day, with the upper confidence limit near 2.2 g per kg per day. Above that, extra protein showed little average added benefit for muscle gain.

## [S4] Estimating BMR with Mifflin-St Jeor
source: Mifflin et al. (1990); Frankenfield et al., J Am Diet Assoc (2005) review
status: unchecked
Resting energy (BMR) is estimated as 10 x weight in kg + 6.25 x height in cm - 5 x age, then +5 for men or -161 for women. Reviews found it predicts resting energy better than older equations such as Harris-Benedict for most adults, but individual errors of 10% or more still happen. Daily needs are BMR times a lifestyle factor, plus exercise.

## [S5] Estimating exercise calories with METs
source: Compendium of Physical Activities (Ainsworth et al.)
status: unchecked
One MET is resting energy use. Calories burned in a workout are roughly MET x body weight in kg x hours. MET values are group averages, and the true cost varies with fitness, technique and body composition, so workout calorie estimates are rough and can easily be off by 20% or more.

## [S6] Rate of fat loss
source: Common sports-nutrition guidance (e.g. Helms et al., 2014)
status: unchecked
For people who lift weights, a commonly suggested pace of fat loss is about 0.5 to 1% of body weight per week. Faster deficits raise the risk of losing lean mass, reduce training quality and are hard to sustain. Higher protein intake and resistance training help preserve muscle while in a deficit.

## [S7] Calorie surplus for building muscle
source: Common sports-nutrition guidance
status: unchecked
Building muscle usually needs only a modest energy surplus, often about 5 to 10% above maintenance, which is a few hundred kcal per day. Larger surpluses tend to add more body fat without speeding up muscle gain much, especially in experienced lifters. Beginners can sometimes gain muscle close to maintenance calories.

## [S9] Protein for endurance athletes
source: Joint position of ACSM, Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and Dietitians of Canada (2016)
status: unchecked
Athletes in general are advised to eat roughly 1.2 to 2.0 g protein per kg per day. Endurance athletes usually sit in the lower to middle part of that range, while strength athletes and people in a calorie deficit sit toward the upper end. For endurance training, carbohydrate intake matters more than protein for fuelling sessions.

## [S10] Plant-based diets and protein
source: General sports-nutrition guidance
status: unchecked
Plant proteins tend to be lower in some essential amino acids, such as leucine and lysine, and slightly less digestible than animal proteins. Vegetarians and vegans can meet their needs by eating a variety of legumes, soy foods, grains and nuts, and aiming toward the upper part of the protein range gives a safety margin. Soy foods, paneer, curd and whey are practical high-quality options where the diet allows them.

## [S11] Who should not rely on these estimates
source: General safety guidance
status: unchecked
These estimates are for healthy adults. People under 18, who are pregnant or breastfeeding, or who have kidney disease, diabetes, a history of eating disorders, or take medication that affects appetite or metabolism should get individual advice from a doctor or registered dietitian. Aggressive calorie deficits are not advisable for anyone using a general calculator.

## [S12] Training days versus rest days
source: General sports-nutrition guidance
status: unchecked
Protein needs do not change much between training and rest days, because recovery continues for a day or more after a session, so keeping protein steady is common practice. Energy needs vary more: calories are higher on heavy training days, but the weekly average intake matters more than any single day.
